# Create a pitch deck from scratch

Start with who will see the pitch, whether it will be presented live or read independently, and what action the founder wants from the audience. Reuse decisions the user has already made. Carry the brand guide and deck outline into every slide design.

## 1. Write a one-page brand guide

Ask the questions as a worksheet or in short batches. Invite links, screenshots, or uploads as part of the conversation.

1. What's the company called, what does it offer, and where will people encounter the brand first?
2. Who is it for, what problem does it solve, and what should they remember in one sentence?
3. What three words should describe the brand? What three words should people never use to describe it?
4. Can you share a Pinterest board, landing page, deck, or screenshots? What exactly would you borrow or avoid from each reference?
5. Which existing logos, colors, fonts, and images are fixed? Which can change?
6. What roles should background, text, and accent colors play? What type feels right for headlines, body text, and labels?
7. How dense should the layouts feel, where should the eye land first, and what kinds of photography, illustrations, diagrams, or charts belong?
8. How should the brand sound? Give one line that sounds like you.

Inspect the supplied references before describing them. Ask for a screenshot or description when a reference is inaccessible. Save a one-page Markdown guide with the audience, promise, voice, reference notes, approved assets, palette by role, and rules for layout and imagery. Specify font family, weight, and size by role at the chosen slide canvas size. Mark unsettled choices **Proposed** or **Open**.

## 2. Outline the investor story

Use this sequence as a starting point. Write one takeaway headline, one proof point or demo idea, and any material evidence gap per slide.

| Slide | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| 1. Intro | What do you do, for whom, and with what outcome? |
| 2. Problem | Who feels the pain, how do they cope today, and what does it cost? |
| 3. Solution | What changes for the customer, and how does your approach make it possible? |
| 4. Product or demo | What real workflow shows the problem, action, and result? |
| 5. Why now | What changed that makes this opportunity timely? |
| 6. Market | Who buys first, how many are there, and how can that market expand? |
| 7. Competition | What are buyers' alternatives, and on which trade-off do you win? |
| 8. Business model | Who pays, for what, and how is it priced? |
| 9. Go-to-market | How will you reach and convert the next customers? |
| 10. Traction | What dated evidence shows demand or progress? Distinguish paid business, pilots, signed intent, and pipeline. |
| 11. Team | Why is this team equipped to solve this problem? |
| 12. Ask and milestones | What action do you want now, and what milestone will it help reach? For a raise, give the amount and use of funds. For introductions, name the desired buyer profiles. |

For ten slides, combine solution with product proof and business model with go-to-market. Bring traction forward when it makes the case faster. Give the cost of the problem its own slide when it needs room. State sources and assumptions for market estimates and keep detailed calculations in the appendix.

In Apparent's document pitch, customer pain, data context, and cost each got a slide before the solution. Business model came before market sizing because the pricing assumptions fed the estimate. The immediate ask was customer introductions tied to a validation milestone.

## 3. Design and build with Crazy 8s

1. **Define one slide.** Specify its job, exact sourced copy, intended reading order, and what the audience should see that proves the claim. Use real evidence when available and label mockups or illustrative numbers.
2. **Generate eight full-slide directions.** Use one image-generation call per direction. Hold the claim, evidence, and brand rules fixed. Vary layout, focal point, reading path, and how the evidence is shown. Inspect the actual images side by side at equal scale. Replace near-duplicates and reject directions that invent proof or hide important qualifications.
3. **Refine the selected direction.** Record why it works and run a focused refinement before rebuilding it. Start with a representative slide and reuse approved patterns across the deck. Return to divergence when a slide needs a substantially different composition.
4. **Rebuild in the deck's editable format.** Implement the selected composition with real text, assets, and data. Render it in the intended presentation format. Check font family, weight, and size against the guide, along with contrast, color roles, reading order, spacing, overlap, and legibility at presentation size. Compare with the selected concept and adjacent slides. Fix and re-render any issues you observe.

## 4. Give visual feedback with Apparent

- Open the draft slide and record a short Apparent capture. Point at or mark the exact area while saying where your eye goes, where it should go, and what the revision should preserve. For example, “My eye lands on the photo. Make it quieter so I read the headline first, then the two costs. Keep their amounts and units.”
- Share the capture ID or local bundle with the agent and identify the slide/version. Run `apparent inspect <capture-id-or-bundle>` when the CLI is available. Review the summary, contact sheet, and relevant full-size frames before editing. Resolve references like “this” from the marks and frames. If capture access is blocked, ask for the relevant annotated screenshot and spoken note or transcript.
- Turn each actionable note into a target and an expected visible result. Render the revision and compare before and after at the same scale. Add feedback that applies across slides to the brand guide, then check affected slides against the updated rule.

Deliver the editable deck, presentation export if requested, brand guide, and outline. Identify any evidence gaps or changes that still need review.
